\chapter{CTL over Generated Futures}
\label{ch:ctl}

\index{CTL}
\index{temporal logic}
\index{LTL}
\index{modal logic}
A grammar generates a branching transition system. CTL speaks directly about
that branching, but its vocabulary becomes clearer when placed between basic
modal logic and linear temporal logic. Path quantifiers distinguish \emph{there
exists a future} from \emph{every future}, while temporal operators distinguish
the next state, eventuality, invariance, and until.

The author's modal-logic background used here was learned mostly from
\citet{kroger2008temporal}. This citation identifies the source of that education;
it is not a claim that the authors originated modal or temporal logic.

\section{Basic modal logic}

Propositional logic evaluates a formula at one world or state. Basic modal logic
adds an accessibility relation $R$ between worlds and two operators:

\begin{align*}
  w \models \Box p &\quad\text{iff}\quad
    \text{$p$ holds at every $v$ such that $wRv$},\\
  w \models \Diamond p &\quad\text{iff}\quad
    \text{$p$ holds at some $v$ such that $wRv$}.
\end{align*}

The readings are ``necessarily $p$'' and ``possibly $p$.'' Under classical
negation they are dual: $\Diamond p \equiv \neg\Box\neg p$. The symbols do not
fix the meaning of accessibility. Depending on the model, an accessible world
may be a next computation state, a world compatible with an actor's knowledge,
a normatively permitted world, or something else.

The basic normal modal logic K allows arbitrary Kripke frames. Additional frame
conditions validate additional principles: reflexivity, transitivity, symmetry,
and the Euclidean condition lead to familiar stronger systems. Those principles
must not be imported merely because the English word ``necessarily'' sounds
strong. LeanFM uses a directed computation-successor relation and does not
silently treat it as reflexive, symmetric, or epistemic.

\section{LTL: time along one path}

Linear Temporal Logic (LTL) describes one linear path with operators including
$X$ (next), $F$ (eventually), $G$ (always), and $U$ (until). Thus $F p$ says that
$p$ occurs at some suffix of the selected path, while $G p$ says that $p$ holds
at every suffix of that path.

LTL's temporal words answer \emph{where along this path}; they do not themselves
choose between one possible branch and every branch of a transition system. A
model-checking convention often asks whether every initial path satisfies an LTL
formula. That outer convention should not be confused with a path quantifier in
the LTL syntax. For example, asking whether some legal execution eventually
reaches recovery and asking whether all legal executions eventually reach
recovery require different branching claims even though both contain the words
``eventually recovery.''

LeanFM does not currently implement LTL. It uses CTL because the requirements
deliberately combine two independent axes:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{always} or \emph{eventually} says where along forward time a
        predicate must hold; and
  \item \emph{necessarily} or \emph{possibly} says whether it must hold on every
        permitted forward continuation or may hold on at least one.
\end{itemize}

This choice arose from trying to combine temporal logic with alethic logic while
investigating whether all the familiar modal families could be combined. The
construction retained by LeanFM is specifically \emph{temporally alethic}:
possible means possible now or at some point along a legal future. Ordinary
language uses ``always'' both for universal modality and for temporal persistence.
CTL keeps those coordinates visible long enough to prevent the ambiguity from
disappearing inside an English gloss.

\section{CTL: path modality combined with time}

CTL pairs a path quantifier with each temporal operator. $E$ is existential:
there is at least one permitted continuation. $A$ is universal: every permitted
continuation satisfies the temporal condition. In the vocabulary of modal
logic, $E$ supplies temporal possibility and $A$ supplies temporal necessity.

``Necessary'' and ``possible'' are therefore future-relative readings here.
They quantify over computations from the current observation, not over timeless
logical worlds. The four most important combinations can be read literally:

\begin{align*}
 EF\,p &: \text{possibly eventually $p$}, &
 AF\,p &: \text{necessarily eventually $p$},\\
 EG\,p &: \text{possibly always $p$}, &
 AG\,p &: \text{necessarily always $p$}.
\end{align*}

The temporal word and modal word do different work. $F$ versus $G$ chooses
eventuality versus invariance \emph{along} a continuation. $E$ versus $A$
chooses some versus all permitted continuations. Neither axis should be omitted
when stating a branching-time requirement.

For requirements argument, the four formulas also form a useful modal square:

\begin{align*}
 EF\,p &: \text{maybe possible $p$}, &
 AG\,p \equiv \neg EF\,\neg p &: \text{always necessary $p$},\\
 AF\,p &: \text{always possible $p$}, &
 EG\,p \equiv \neg AF\,\neg p &: \text{maybe always $p$}.
\end{align*}

These are exact CTL dualities, not merely slogans. They expose two meanings
often hidden by ``always'': $A$ ranges over all legal futures, while $G$ ranges
over all positions along one future. Calling necessity ``always'' without naming
the coordinate loses information precisely where a requirements argument needs
it.

The dualities are also computationally central. LeanFM need not establish a
universal claim by enumerating every complete future. It searches for an
existential counterexample:

\begin{align*}
 AG\,p &\equiv \neg EF\,\neg p, &
 AF\,p &\equiv \neg EG\,\neg p.
\end{align*}

Thus a safety proof searches for a reachable violation of $p$, and a universal
eventuality proof searches for a path on which $\neg p$ persists forever. A
counterexample is a witness; absence of such a witness in the finite checked
model establishes the corresponding universal result. Keeping the exact CTL
form is what permits this reduction from universal checking to existential
witness search through negation.

An observation is immutable once recorded. A value changes only by moving
forward to a successor observation; it is never updated retroactively inside the
current state. This does not mean that values are monotone---a queue may grow and
later shrink---but every change occurs on a forward transition.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
Formula & Reading & Typical use \\
\midrule
$EF\,p$ & possibly, eventually $p$ & temporal possibility / witness \\
$AF\,p$ & necessarily, eventually $p$ & termination / response \\
$EG\,p$ & possibly, always $p$ & viable invariant path \\
$AG\,p$ & necessarily, always $p$ & safety invariant \\
$EX\,p$ & possibly, next $p$ & enabled successor / one-step witness \\
$AX\,p$ & necessarily, next $p$ & required one-step consequence \\

$(p\ EU\ q)$ & some path keeps $p$ until $q$ & possible progress \\
$(p\ AW\ q)$ & every path: $p$ until $q$, or $p$ forever & necessary weak until \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

For example, $AF\,p$ is not merely ``eventually $p$'' and $EF\,p$ is not a
weaker spelling of the same linear claim. The first requires $p$ on every legal
future generated from the current trace prefix; the second needs one legal
future as a witness. Branching is part of the requirement.

Strict CTL requires every temporal operator to be governed by $A$ or $E$. CTL*
is a more general branching-time language in which state formulas and path
formulas can be nested more freely; LeanFM does not implement CTL*. This matters
when translating prose: not every arbitrary alternation of ``on all paths'' and
``eventually'' is a formula of the small CTL datatype used here.

\section{Projecting to LTL-style F and G}

Requirements often want the simpler LTL surface forms $F p$ and $G p$. LeanFM
may display or accept those forms only with an explicit path-policy boundary.
On a selected linear execution, the branching coordinate has already been fixed,
so $AF p$ and $EF p$ both project to $F p$, while $AG p$ and $EG p$ both project
to $G p$. Under the common model-checking convention that an LTL formula must
hold on every initial path, $F p$ corresponds to $AF p$ and $G p$ corresponds to
$AG p$.

Consequently, presenting frequently used $AF$ and $EG$ obligations as $F$ and
$G$ is a deliberate interface reduction, not a CTL equivalence on a branching
model. The durable artifact retains the exact originating CTL formula so the
checker can still use existential witnesses, negation, and the correct
counterexample search.

\section{General and unresolved modalities}

General modal logic need not have only one accessibility relation. A multimodal
model can use $\Box_t/\Diamond_t$ for temporal reachability,
$K_a/\widehat K_a$ for what actor $a$ knows or considers possible, and a
different pair for permission or obligation. Dynamic logic can index modalities
by programs. Tense logics can add past-directed relations. These systems share
the Kripke-style idea of evaluating modal formulas through accessibility, but
their modalities are not interchangeable.

LeanFM's CTL $A$ and $E$ range only over legal forward computations generated by
the current model. They do not mean:

\begin{itemize}
  \item probability one versus positive probability;
  \item known versus merely believed by an actor;
  \item obligatory versus permitted by a policy; or
  \item physically necessary versus logically possible.
\end{itemize}

The original investigation also sought exact combinations for belief and
knowledge, and for permitted and required behavior. LeanFM does not yet have
accepted formulas or proof rules for those modalities. It therefore must not
encode $E/A$ as if they meant believed/known or permitted/required. For now,
probability, epistemic claims, authorization, and normative requirements need
separately named predicates or future modal extensions. Keeping that absence
explicit prevents an easy category error: a branch can be temporally alethically
possible while having unknown probability, being unknown to an actor, or being
forbidden by policy.

\section{Possibility means preparedness}

There is a practical consequence to treating possibility as a first-class
temporal mode. When a requirement declares $EF\,p$---``maybe possible
$p$''---it does more than acknowledge that a model checker can find an accidental
path to $p$. It says that $p$ is a supported future for which the system must
prepare.

A \texttt{possibly} obligation therefore contains three linked artifacts:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item a legal grammar branch and witness trace reaching the condition;
  \item a handling plan in \texttt{Requirements.lean}; and
  \item generated implementation code whose justification cites that
        possibility requirement.
\end{enumerate}

For example, ``a bakery delivery may fail'' requires a decodable
\texttt{DeliveryFailed} message, a legal failure branch, and code that performs
the specified refund or notification behavior. Removing every failure branch
does not implement reliability; it makes the real failure trace out-of-spec.

Possibility is distinct from probability. A possible condition may have a small
estimated probability, an expected distribution, or no known probability at
all. Conversely, assigning positive probability to a condition makes its branch
possible. The handling obligation comes from supported possibility, not from
whether the estimated percentage crosses a threshold.

The dual operational obligation applies to $EG\,p$---``maybe always $p$.'' It
identifies at least one future on which $p$ persists. The system must pay attention
to the conditions that keep that future viable: monitor them, expose their loss,
and, where required, control choices that would destroy the invariant path.
$EG\,p$ alone does not promise that every execution preserves $p$; that stronger
claim is $AG\,p$.

\section{Strong implication and the Chewbacca argument}

The name ``Chewbacca defense'' is a cultural reference to the \emph{South Park}
episode ``Chef Aid,'' whose courtroom parody uses an irrelevant claim about
Chewbacca to distract from the conclusion at issue. The reference supplies a
memorable example of non sequitur; it is not a technical term originating in
modal or temporal logic.

Material implication is weak in the precise sense that
$p\mathbin{\to}q$ is true whenever $p$ is false. That is useful inside a state,
but it permits a vacuous global claim when no reachable state ever satisfies the
antecedent. LeanFM names the non-vacuous temporal combination

\[
  p\mathbin{\Rightarrow_s}q
  \quad\stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\quad
  EF\,p \;\land\; AG(p\mathbin{\to}q).
\]

The first conjunct requires a legal future witnessing $p$. The second requires
that, necessarily always, every reachable occurrence of $p$ also satisfies $q$.
Together they imply $EF\,q$: the $p$ witness cannot avoid $q$.

The deliberately absurd test uses

\begin{align*}
  p &= \text{``Chewbacca lives on Endor''},\\
  q &= \text{``you must acquit''}.
\end{align*}

A Chewbacca argument cannot obtain $q$ merely by mentioning $p$. To enter the
requirements as strong implication, the model must admit a reachable $p$ witness
and establish $AG(p\to q)$ over every permitted continuation. If the observable
grammar supplies no relation between residence and acquittal, that second
obligation has no justification and is rejected. Logic cannot determine the
meaning or relevance of arbitrary English propositions by itself; the useful
point is that neither a missing antecedent nor a single rhetorical occurrence of
it can make the formal implication pass vacuously.

In Lean the definition is direct:

\begin{lstlisting}
def stronglyImplies (p q : CTL S) : CTL S :=
  CTL.and (CTL.ef p) (CTL.ag (CTL.implies p q))
\end{lstlisting}

The path quantifier is part of the until operator. We write \texttt{AU} and
\texttt{EU} as binary algebraic operators, not an outer \texttt{A} or \texttt{E}
wrapped around a bracketed until formula. Consequently a subexpression never
changes meaning merely because another expression surrounds it:

\begin{lstlisting}
def boundedUntilTerminal : CTL Observation :=
  atom withinCapacity AU atom isTerminal

def someAuthorizedContinuation : CTL Observation :=
  atom hasValidAuth EU atom isSucceeded
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Weak until: AW and EW}

Strong until requires the right operand eventually to occur. Weak until permits
it never to occur, provided the left operand continues to hold. Thus

\begin{align*}
  p\ AW\ q &: \text{on every future, $p$ holds until $q$, or $p$ remains forever},\\
  p\ EW\ q &: \text{on some future, $p$ holds until $q$, or $p$ remains forever}.
\end{align*}

The boundary state satisfying $q$ need not also satisfy $p$; $p$ is required
only before $q$. On a future where $q$ never occurs, every state must satisfy
$p$. This makes weak until useful for requirements such as ``necessarily remain
available until shutdown, while shutdown is permitted but not required.''

For existential paths, $p\ EW\ q$ is equivalent to
$(p\ EU\ q)\lor EG\,p$. The analogous-looking
$(p\ AU\ q)\lor AG\,p$ is \emph{not} a correct definition of $AW$ on a branching
system. Some futures may reach $q$ while other futures preserve $p$ forever;
neither global disjunct need hold even though every individual future satisfies
weak until. LeanFM therefore implements \texttt{AW} directly.

\begin{lstlisting}
def serviceMayRunUntilShutdown : CTL Observation :=
  atom available AW atom shutdown

def someWorkerCanRemainReady : CTL Observation :=
  atom ready EW atom retired
\end{lstlisting}

This convention also makes tree structure explicit. In
\texttt{r \&\& (p AU q)}, the right operand is the complete \texttt{AU} expression;
there is no context-sensitive reinterpretation of \texttt{[p U q]}.

For a request event carrying identifier $r$, an expected response property might
say that on every continuation a matching success or failure eventually occurs.
Authentication might say that every successful use of a session has an earlier
issuance event with the same session identifier.

\section{Past claims}

\index{past-time logic}
CTL is future-facing at the current state, but security and protocol claims often
refer to evidence in the past: ``this capability was previously issued'' or ``this
response matches an earlier request.'' Since states are trace prefixes, past facts
can initially be expressed as predicates over the accumulated trace. This gives a
clear baseline without prematurely committing the surface language to a particular
past-time temporal logic.

There is still an open design question. Explicit past operators may make properties
more readable and allow better checking algorithms. The project should compare
trace predicates, CTL with past operators, and compilation of finite-history
monitors into state. The criterion is maintainability of specifications as the
event schema evolves.

\section{Fairness and time bounds}

$AF$ does not by itself distinguish a participant that is perpetually denied a
turn from one that violates the protocol. Fairness assumptions must therefore be
named, scoped, and visible in theorem statements. Similarly, ``eventually'' is not
``within 200 milliseconds.'' Quantitative bounds belong in an explicit timed layer
rather than being smuggled into timestamps and informal prose.
