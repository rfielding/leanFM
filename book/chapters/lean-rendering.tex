\chapter{Lean as the Rendered Language}
\label{ch:lean}

\index{Lean}
The user-facing notation should render to a conservative subset of Lean. There is
no second opaque specification file: domain declarations are Lean definitions,
expected behaviours are Lean values, and requirements are Lean propositions or
checked queries.

Throughout this book, every displayed mathematical formula is accompanied by a
literal English reading that accounts for each operator, index, bound, and new
glyph.  The reading is part of the notation discipline: it must map back to the
symbols one-to-one rather than merely paraphrase the formula's intent.

\begin{lstlisting}
def checkout : Grammar CheckoutEvent :=
  request >>> (accepted <||> rejected)

def eventuallyAnswered : CTL Observation :=
  CTL.ag (CTL.implies requestPending
    (CTL.af (CTL.or requestAccepted requestRejected)))
\end{lstlisting}

Most semantic machinery should be imported from a fixed library. A system author
normally supplies only:

\begin{itemize}
  \item typed actors, identifiers, event kinds, and payloads;
  \item parameterised grammar definitions;
  \item projections from trace prefixes to observable propositions;
  \item named temporal requirements and proofs or decidable checks.
\end{itemize}

This boundary matters for automation. Generated Lean remains small enough to
review, while library proofs and algorithms are reused rather than regenerated.

\section{A Ramanujan-inspired checked discrete account of
  \texorpdfstring{$1+2+3+\cdots=-1/12$}{1+2+3+...=-1/12}}

Lean is not limited to validating data structures or executing finite checks.
This section starts from the less formal result associated with Srinivasa
Ramanujan. What is different here is not the value $-1/12$, but the deliberately
elementary route used to make that value precise and checkable. The construction
does not invoke the Riemann zeta function, analytic continuation, complex
analysis, or a theorem identifying $\zeta(-1)$. Its final proof is exact rational
algebra over explicitly defined finite prefixes and correction tails.

The repository's \texttt{book/logic.lean} uses Mathlib to define the real
function $A(x)=1/(1-x)$ and prove that its derivative is
$F(x)=1/(1-x)^2$ away from the singularity at $x=1$.  It then moves from
that analytic motivation to an exact, discrete construction over rational
numbers.  This construction explains precisely what the file means by the
famous shorthand
\[
  S=1+2+3+\cdots=-\frac{1}{12}.
\]
Read this as: ``$S$, defined by the regularized expression one plus two plus
three and so on, equals negative one twelfth.''  The qualification
``regularized'' is essential; the later formula defines what the ellipsis
means here.

The derivative establishes only the origin of the chosen rational function
$F$; it is not an appeal to zeta regularization. After that motivation, the
definitions of \texttt{Tail\_B}, \texttt{Sum\_B}, \texttt{Tail\_S}, and
\texttt{Sum\_S}, and the proof of the final identity, use discrete identities
and rational arithmetic. Thus the checked claim stands on the definitions
printed here rather than importing a higher-level theory that already assigns
a value to the divergent series.

The complete source file follows, without elision.  The listing starts on this
page and continues onto the next page through the final theorem

checked source, including Lean's $\land$ notation; the listing configuration maps those

The hypothesis \texttt{hx : x != 1} is not commentary: it is required to prove
that the denominator is nonzero. The proof composes Mathlib's derivative rule
for the identity function with subtraction and reciprocal, extracts the proved
derivative, unfolds the local definitions, and asks the simplifier to match the
resulting expression. Lean checks every type conversion and proof obligation;
removing the singularity condition leaves the reciprocal rule unjustified.

The discrete part begins with the ordinary finite triangular sum
\[
  \texttt{Sum\_S}(n)=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}.
\]
Read this as: ``\texttt{Sum\_S} at cutoff $n$ equals $n$ times $n+1$, divided
by two.''  Here $n$ is a natural-number parameter naming the finite cutoff.
It then defines an exact rational correction \texttt{Tail\_S}(n), using the
alternating construction \texttt{Tail\_B}, so that the corrected value is
independent of where the finite prefix is cut.  The last theorem states, for
\emph{every} natural number $n$,
\[
  -\frac{1}{12}=\frac{n(n+1)}{2}+\texttt{Tail\_S}(n).
\]
Read this as: ``negative one twelfth equals $n$ times $n+1$, divided by two,
plus \texttt{Tail\_S} at that same cutoff $n$.''
Thus the ellipsis in $1+2+3+\cdots$ is given explicit content: retain a finite
prefix and pair it with the particular tail assigned by the preceding
self-similarity identities.  Moving the cutoff changes both terms but not their
sum.  This is a discrete, algebraically checked account of the regularized
value $-1/12$; it is not the false claim that the ordinary partial sums of
positive integers converge to a negative number.  Lean makes that distinction
hard to blur because the exact proposition---including the tail and the
quantification over $n$---is visible in Listing~\ref{lst:logic-lean}.

At the end, Lean's goal is the fully elaborated proposition

\begin{lstlisting}[language=Lean]
⊢ ∀ (n : ℕ), -1 / 12 = ↑n * (↑n + 1) / 2 + Tail_S n
\end{lstlisting}

Read the Lean goal one symbol at a time as: ``prove, for every $n$ of type
natural number, that negative one divided by twelve equals coerced $n$ times
coerced $n$ plus one, divided by two, plus \texttt{Tail\_S} applied to $n$.''
The upward arrows are coercions: they convert the natural number $n$ into the
rational-number type used by the arithmetic around it.

The single turnstile $\vdash$, pronounced ``proves'' or ``derives,'' separates
the local proof context on its left from the proposition to be derived on its
right.  A context may contain assumptions, variables, local definitions, and
previously established facts.  Here nothing is printed to the left, so there
are no additional local entries: Lean is asking for a term that derives the
universally quantified proposition from the imported theory and the definitions
above.

By contrast, the double turnstile $\vDash$, pronounced ``semantically
entails,'' describes truth in interpretations rather than the construction of a
proof term.  Read
\[
  \Gamma \vDash p
\]
as: ``Gamma semantically entails $p$.''  Symbol for symbol, $\Gamma$ is the
theory or context---its assumptions, definitions, and axioms; $\vDash$ is the
semantic-entailment relation; and $p$ is the proposition.  The whole statement
means that every interpretation satisfying every entry in $\Gamma$ also
satisfies $p$.  The two readings therefore remain distinct: $\Gamma\vdash p$
says that $p$ is derivable from the context, while $\Gamma\vDash p$ says that
$p$ is true in every model of the context.

For the proof-theoretic background behind derivations, natural deduction,
sequent calculus, normalization, and cut elimination, see Mancosu, Galvan, and
Zach's \emph{An Introduction to Proof Theory} \citep{mancosu2021proof}.

The completed proof establishes the displayed proposition for every $n$.  The
\texttt{def} declarations define the mathematics: the finite sums, correction
tails, and the values assembled from them.  The \texttt{theorem} declarations
then state claims about those definitions and require proofs that Lean checks.
Thus the final theorem is not a numerical experiment over a selection of
cutoffs.  It verifies the universally quantified identity that results from
the definitions.

\section{From specification to service}

Lean can host executable code, including a small web server. That makes the formal
artifact part of the running project rather than a document beside it. The same
typed definitions can drive validation endpoints, test-trace generation, protocol
schemas, visualisations, and runtime monitors. Extraction and integration choices
must preserve the distinction between a verified behavioural boundary and an
unverified infrastructure layer.
