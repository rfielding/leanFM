\chapter{Session Types as a Software Engineering Method}
\label{ch:vision}

\index{session type}
\index{session type!multiparty}
\index{formal methods}
LeanFM is a session-type method for engineering software. A \emph{multiparty
session type} is a global description of a conversation: which roles take part,
which messages pass between them, in what order, and where it branches
\citep{honda2008multiparty}. Each role's \emph{local type} is derived from it by
projection, and a role built to its local type cannot send a message nobody
expects, or wait forever for one nobody will send. That is the contract a team
needs at every boundary where it does not want to read the other side's code.

Session types began as a type discipline for programming languages
\citep{honda1993types,honda1998language}. LeanFM uses them one level up, as the
requirements: the global type is what stakeholders argue about, the local types
are what each implementer owes, and recorded production events are checked
against both.

LeanFM departs from the textbook theory in one deliberate way: \emph{its channels
have physics}. Standard asynchronous session types assume every message arrives
exactly once, in order, through a queue that never fills. Real channels are
bounded; UDP drops and reorders; a timed-out HTTP call is retried and seen
twice; a Kafka consumer replays a batch after a rebalance; a timer cannot tell a
slow answer from a lost one. LeanFM makes each channel's capacity and failure
behaviour part of the requirement and checks the protocol together with it.
Chapter~\ref{ch:tftp-physics} shows the consequence: a grammar that is a perfect
session type, and an implementation with a famous bug that appears only once the
physics is declared. Messages between network nodes and calls between APIs are
handled the same way; they are channels that differ only in their physics.

\noindent\textbf{Evidence labels used throughout:} proposed notation (uncompiled);
executable code (builds or runs, not proof); finite-model check (exhaustive only
within declared bounds); test (named finite cases); generated analysis (named
inputs and reducer); Lean proof (kernel-checked under stated assumptions); future
interface (planned, unavailable). Labels scope evidence; several may apply to one
artifact.

The theory asks two questions of a global type, and so does LeanFM.

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
  \item[One chooser per choice.] Every branch must be started by the same role,
  which is the one that decides. If Client could start one branch while Shop
  starts another, both could act at once and leave the protocol. This is the
  realizability (non-local choice) condition.
  \item[Everyone who must know, learns.] A role whose later behaviour differs
  between branches must be told which branch was taken. In the \texttt{soldOut}
  branch of \texttt{Purchase} the bank is never told anything.
\end{description}

Classic projection rejects \texttt{Purchase} for that. LeanFM accepts it, for a
reason that comes from how servers are built: a role whose part in the session
has not started owes nothing to a branch that never contacts it. The bank's part
starts when \texttt{pay} arrives, as a thread forked for that session; in the
\texttt{soldOut} branch that thread never exists, so checking it on its own is
exact. The bank's projection shows the empty branch:

\index{communicating finite-state machines}
Bounded queues are also what makes exhaustive checking possible. Roles exchanging
messages through unbounded FIFO queues are communicating finite-state machines,
for which most verification questions are undecidable
\citep{brand1983communicating}. Session types avoid this by restricting the
protocol's shape \citep{denielou2012communicating}; LeanFM instead bounds the
queues. Finite roles and finite queues give a finite transition system, so every
CTL question is answered exhaustively, with a witness or a counterexample.

\section{Every boundary is a channel}

\index{API boundary}
\index{channel declaration}
A channel is any path a message can take between two roles. It declares its
physics along six axes (\textbf{proposed notation}; the TFTP chapter builds its
UDP channels by hand today).

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{.17\textwidth}p{.3\textwidth}p{.43\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Axis & Values & What it adds to the checked product \\
\midrule
capacity & a positive number of messages & a send to a full channel takes the \emph{when full} policy \\
when full & block, reject, drop newest, drop oldest & block: the sender waits; reject: the sender receives an observable refusal (an HTTP 503 or 429); the drops lose a message silently \\
ordering & FIFO, per key, none & which queued message may be delivered next \\
sharding & none, or a key field and a partition count & the channel becomes several queues; ordering holds within one partition only \\
loss & never, may drop & a transition that removes a queued message \\
duplication & never, may duplicate & a transition that delivers a message and leaves a copy queued \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Most channels are one of a few familiar kinds, named as presets. A declaration
starts from one and overrides what differs, as in \texttt{udp (capacity := 2)}.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{.13\textwidth}p{.16\textwidth}p{.6\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Preset & Typical use & Physics \\
\midrule
\texttt{reliable} & internal APIs, in-process calls, Go channels & FIFO, never lost, never duplicated, blocks when full \\
\texttt{http} & calls between services & each request and its response may be lost, so the caller has a deadline; a retried request may arrive twice; concurrent requests have no order; a busy server rejects \\
\texttt{udp} & datagrams & no order, may drop, may duplicate, drops the newest message when full \\
\texttt{stream} & TCP, TLS, websockets & FIFO and never lost while open; it may close after any message, and both sides observe the close as the end of the session \\
\texttt{kafka} & event topics & sharded by a key field; FIFO within a partition; never lost within retention; delivered at least once, so may duplicate; the producer blocks when full \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Physics never appears inside the global type. The type is written as if every
channel were \texttt{reliable} (the default for an undeclared channel), with the
declarations beside it, one line per channel. A lossy channel costs lines in the
type only where the protocol must answer it: a timer and a resend for
\texttt{udp}, a deadline and a retry for \texttt{http}. Declaring a channel
\texttt{udp} without those lines makes the protocol wrong, and the checker says
so. Most internal boundaries are \texttt{reliable}; the few that cross a network
are where the physics, and the bugs, live.

\texttt{tls} is a modifier, not a preset: \texttt{tls http} or \texttt{tls
stream} adds an authenticated peer identity to every message and changes nothing
else.

\index{sharding}
A sharded channel is several queues; a message goes to the partition chosen by
hashing its key field, and order holds only between messages with the same key.
A session keeps its order across a sharded channel only if all its messages carry
the same key, such as the session identifier. LeanFM can check this from the
declarations alone: a global type that relies on two messages arriving in order,
over a channel sharded by a field that differs between them, is rejected before
any state is explored.

\subsection{Masked and surfaced failures}

\index{failure!masked}
\index{failure!surfaced}
A lower layer can hide a failure by retrying, and the failure becomes latency.
TCP retransmits, so a protocol over a \texttt{stream} sees no loss, only delay,
until the connection closes. UDP hides nothing, so a protocol over it handles
loss, reordering and duplicates itself, as TFTP does. Kafka hides loss inside
its retention window and surfaces duplicates instead. Every interface kind says
which conditions it \emph{surfaces}, which the protocol must handle, and which it
\emph{masks} as latency, which the protocol's deadlines must allow for.

\subsection{The deployment is a tree}

\index{island}
\index{topology}
\index{naming!implicit}
Where the two ends of a channel run adds the conditions of the network between
them and decides whether they may talk at all. LeanFM describes a deployment as
one tree: islands that may be partitioned from each other, datacenters and
machines inside them, groups for replicated and sharded roles, actors, and under
each actor its interfaces grouped by \emph{scope}. A node's name is its path
(\textbf{proposed notation}; \texttt{TopologyExample.lean} holds the same tree
as Lean values):

\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
world {
  outside { browser }
  us {
    datacenter dc1 {
      machine m1 { alice { ingress { https }  internal { api } } }
      replicated x3 by raft { ledger { internal { api } } }
    }
  }
  eu {
    datacenter dc1 {
      machine m1 { bob { ingress { http }  peering { api } } }
      machine m2 { charles { ingress { https }  internal { api } }
                   audit   { local { syslog } } }
    }
  }
  sharded by account across { us, eu } { accounts { peering { topic } } }
}
\end{lstlisting}

The implicit name of an interface keeps the island, the actor, the scope and the
interface, and leaves out the containers between them:
\texttt{us.alice.ingress.https}, \texttt{eu.audit.local.syslog}. Moving
\texttt{alice} to another machine in \texttt{us} does not rename her.

\paragraph{A local type is a production.} Projecting the global type onto one
node of the tree gives that node's local type, an ordinary production of the
grammar, cut off where the node's tasks leave its boundary: a call to another
node is one terminal, \texttt{call eu: ship(order) returns shipped}, and how
\texttt{eu} produces \texttt{shipped} is \texttt{eu}'s own production. Folding
the crossings above, with the tree cut at its top level:

\input{generated/topology-local-types.tex}

Each production can be read as a top-level protocol, so requirements stack:
\texttt{us} is specified against the promises of \texttt{eu} and
\texttt{us+eu}, not their insides. Opening a node replaces its production by its
children's, whose crossings must fold back into the parent's. A team can agree
on the islands view first and refine each island later; a refinement is
acceptable when collapsing it again gives back the agreed view.

Placement is an implementation decision, so the tree's containers belong in
\texttt{Implementation.lean}; the scopes and what each channel's protocol handles
are requirements. Moving \texttt{bob} into \texttt{us} would remove the partition
from channel~5; moving \texttt{ledger} out of \texttt{us} would make channel~4
unreachable. Either is a checked change, acceptable only if no channel turns red
after \texttt{lake env lean --run TopologyExample.lean}.

With channel declarations, inter-API design is network-protocol design. A browser
call that passes a reverse proxy to a service is a three-role session:

\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
q: tls http GET /users/{id}       from Browser to Proxy;
r: http GET /users/{id}           from Proxy to Users   cause q
     where header USER_DN = peer.dn(q);
s: http 200 {user}                from Users to Proxy   cause r;
   http 200 {user}                from Proxy to Browser cause s;
\end{lstlisting}

The requirement names only what crosses a boundary. Whether \texttt{Users}
answers from a cache, a database or a replica is left free by not being written
down. Negative requirements follow the same rule: ``a request without a client
certificate never reaches \texttt{Users}'' is a statement about observable
messages on two channels, checked and tested as one.

\subsection{Cut and un-cut: what HTTP allows}

\index{cut}
\index{HTTP!call discipline}
A global type may say that a reply comes from someone other than the role that
was asked:

\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
fq: X() from A to B;
fr: Y() from C to A   cause fq;
\end{lstlisting}

Over HTTP this cannot happen: a response travels back on its request's
connection. The \texttt{http} channel kind therefore adds a rule: every message
is either a request to an interface of its receiver, or the response to a pending
request its receiver sent to its sender. A protocol that breaks the rule must be
\emph{un-cut}, as a proof with a cut is expanded into one without: the missing
calls and returns are written out.

\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
fq:  X()  from A to B;
fbc: X'() from B to C   cause fq;
fcb: Y'() from C to B   cause fbc;
fr:  Y()  from B to A   cause fcb;
\end{lstlisting}

Collapse \texttt{B} and \texttt{C} into one lifeline, as in
\cref{fig:topology-islands}, and both forms become an ordinary call and return
between \texttt{A} and \texttt{BC}. A cut is a statement made at a coarser zoom
level than its names suggest, sound exactly when the roles it jumps between sit
inside one node that the caller sees as a single HTTP server. \texttt{Purchase}
is cut twice: \texttt{paid} answers the client's payment to the shop, and
\texttt{shipped} answers a request the client never made. Un-cut for HTTP, with
the client as the only role that calls the shop:

\begin{lstlisting}[language={}]
o: http POST /orders {item}          from Client to Shop;
choice {
  q: http 200 {quote}                from Shop to Client  cause o;
  p: http POST /payments {quote}     from Client to Bank  cause q;
  r: http 201 {receipt}              from Bank to Client  cause p;
  c: http POST /orders/{id} {receipt} from Client to Shop cause r;
  v: http POST /receipts/verify      from Shop to Bank    cause c;
  w: http 200 {paid}                 from Bank to Shop    cause v;
     http 200 {shipped}              from Shop to Client  cause w;
} or {
     http 409 {soldOut}              from Shop to Client  cause o;
}
\end{lstlisting}

Every \texttt{cause} now names a message its sender received, and every response
goes back to the role that asked. The other un-cut is a callback, which needs the
shop to expose an interface the bank's island may reach
(\cref{tab:topology-channels}). Choosing is a design decision; LeanFM's job is to
reject the cut form on an \texttt{http} channel and show both (a \textbf{future
interface}; the rule above is its definition).

\section{Choice is always internal}

\index{internal choice}
\index{realizability}
Session-type theory distinguishes \emph{internal} choice (a role selects which
label to send) from \emph{external} choice (a role offers to receive any of
several). Among actors reading their own queues only the first is a decision: an
external choice is the queue delivering what another role decided. So every
choice is made by one role, from what it can know: the messages it has sent and
received.

Every recorded event carries \texttt{priorIds}, the earlier events it follows,
and \texttt{causeId}, the one event that made it happen (Chapter~\ref{ch:events}).
Realizability is then a rule about pointers: \emph{a role's event may
cause-point only to events that role sent or received.} A protocol in which a
role reacts to something it never saw cannot be implemented, and is rejected (a
\textbf{future interface}; the rule defines the validation).

\section{The requirement is the global type}

The requirement is the set of permitted, visible histories: who communicated with
whom, which message, what it carried, and which earlier messages it follows.
Message bytes and persistent formats become compatibility boundaries once another
process, customer or stored data set depends on them. Threads, actors, caches and
retry machinery inside a role are realization choices unless they change the
observable conversation; modelling them couples requirements to harmless
refactoring and multiplies the state space. Channel capacity and failure
behaviour, by contrast, \emph{are} observable, because they decide which
histories can occur.

\section{Comparison with TLA+}

TLA+ is the specification language most engineers meet first. LeanFM neither
extends nor replaces it. A TLA+ specification commonly has variables, an
initial-state predicate, a next-state relation, and temporal formulas over the
resulting behaviours \citep{lamport2002specifying}; a message queue is a
sequence-valued variable that actions append to and remove from.

\texttt{Next} is a relation between the current variables and their primed next
values: one step is a \texttt{Send}, \texttt{Receive}, \texttt{Finish},
\texttt{ReadReply}, cache step or clock step, and an action whose guards are false
is disabled. \verb|Spec == Init /\ [][Next]_vars| reads: the first state
satisfies \texttt{Init}, and every later step satisfies \texttt{Next} or leaves
\texttt{vars} unchanged. \texttt{inbox'} is the next value of \texttt{inbox};
\texttt{Append}, \texttt{Head} and \texttt{Tail} add, select and remove a message.
Alice's send updates Bob's inbox, not his control state; Bob's receive does that.
The full module commits the order, replies, and checks that no reply precedes
placement.

\subsection{Proposed message-first notation}

This is invented TLA+-inspired syntax, neither standard TLA+ nor a compiled

\lstinputlisting[language={},basicstyle=\ttfamily\footnotesize]{examples/tla/MessageFirst.proposed}

\texttt{alice:bob ! placeOrder} reads ``Alice sends placeOrder to Bob'' and
\texttt{bob ? placeOrder} ``Bob receives placeOrder''. Inboxes are first-class

routing, failure and scheduling rules (Chapter~\ref{ch:grammar}); it does not
remove their state space.

\subsection{Requirements at the message boundary}

LeanFM starts from the conversation: a global type over decoded production
events, whose order is classified as conforming or a counterexample without
pretending to observe private variables. A property that depends on hidden state
needs an observable event or a justified abstraction; otherwise its status is
indeterminate. This is a workflow choice, not a claim that TLA+ cannot express
queues: our first TLA+-style attempt had to model internal state before an
ordinary observed scenario could be recorded.

\section{Discovery first, consolidation before commitment}

During discovery the book may hold alternatives that are not accepted. Before a
specification is committed, and again before implementation, classify every
normative idea as a requirement, an implementation decision, a deferred item, or
material to remove. Replace prose that mainly enumerates roles, messages, states,
ordering or quantities with a generated view and a short interpretation; each
view keeps its prompt, question, event source, reducer, unit and boundary. Only
committed, checked artifacts are binding.

\section{Four ingredients}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
  \item Session types give the backbone: one global description of a
        conversation, projected to each role's obligations, with well-formedness
        conditions that rule out unimplementable choices
        \citep{honda1998language,honda2008multiparty,yoshida2013scribble}.
  \item Language theory gives compact generative descriptions of legal
        histories: a global type is a grammar whose terminals are messages.
        Dana Richards and Henry Hamburger, the author's college professors, are
        cited in \citet{richards2017logic} as where the author learned these
        methods, not as their originators; for symbolization, validity, proof,
        quantification and modal reasoning we also draw on
        \citet{gensler2017introduction}.
  \item Protocol analysis contributes explicit participants, adversarial
        choices, trace semantics and tool-supported checking. An attacker is one
        more role, or a channel an adversary controls. The CSP treatment of
        \citet{ryan2001modelling}, including Steve Schneider, is cited as where
        the author learned it.
  \item Temporal logic turns expectations into queries over the product of roles
        and channels. CTL is central because it distinguishes claims about some
        possible future from claims about every one \citep{clarke1986automatic}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{A test for every abstraction}

\begin{quote}
Can a developer state a conversation once, with the physics of its channels,
compose it with other conversations, check its consequences, and keep it stable
while the implementation of each role changes?
\end{quote}

An abstraction that does not improve that workflow does not belong in the core
library.
