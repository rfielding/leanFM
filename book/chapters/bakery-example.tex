The bakery is the quantitative worked example. Its tasks are written as a
\emph{core}, which says only what can happen, plus \emph{overlays}: separate
tables of branch probabilities and delays. A \emph{declared} overlay states
what we expect; a \emph{measured} overlay is reduced from recorded events.
Both answer the same questions about the same unchanged core, and
Chapter~\ref{ch:generated-examples} develops the idea in general. The
corpus is the \BakeryEvents{}-event stream \texttt{examples/bakery-events.jsonl},
a reproducible synthetic population generated by
\texttt{scripts/generate\_bakery\_events.py}; its numbers describe that corpus,
not a real bakery. Every figure and table below is regenerated by
\texttt{lake env lean --run BakeryFlowExample.lean}.

\section{The morning, generated}

Bread is baked to a schedule, not to order. Each batch is baked, queued on a
truck, and stocked at the storefront before opening. The stocking task's core
lists only those four messages; the overlay measured from the 140 recorded
batches supplies how long each step takes. The figure is the most likely
stocking run under that overlay.

\section{A sale: what can happen}

A customer asks for bread. The storefront either reserves stocked inventory or
turns the customer away; a reserved order is paid for, and the payment either
completes the sale or is declined. The core states this and nothing more. It
already proves that every sale attempt ends and that a customer \emph{can} be
turned away.

\section{What we expected, and what the corpus shows}

The declared overlay was written before looking at any data: a 3\% stockout
rate, a 1\% payment-decline rate, and round-number delays. The measured overlay
has exactly the same shape. \texttt{LeanFM.Overlay.measure} walks each of the
20{,}000 recorded sales through the core, counts how often each branch was
taken, and averages the time since the previous message. A recorded session
that left the grammar would be reported rather than counted.

Two rows deserve a closer reading. The corpus contains no stockout in
20{,}000 sales, but ``never observed'' is not ``impossible'': with zero
occurrences in $n$ trials the usual 95\% upper bound on the rate is about
$3/n$, so the honest measured answer is a bound, not zero. The core, meanwhile,
proves that turning a customer away is possible, so the path must still be
designed and tested even though the data never exercised it.

\section{The deadline lesson: a mean hides the spread}

The measured overlay records each step's \emph{mean} delay. Its predicted sale
takes \BakeryMeanSaleMs{}\,ms, inside the 1.2\,s deadline, so the model answers
$P[F_{\le 1200\,\mathrm{ms}}\,\mathit{sold}] = 1$. The recorded sales say
otherwise: only \BakeryWithinDeadline{} of them finished within 1.2\,s. Real
sales took between \BakeryMinSaleMs{} and \BakeryMaxSaleMs{}\,ms.

\begin{bakeryfigure}
\AccessibleFigure{Histogram of the duration of every recorded sale in 100 ms bins. Durations spread from about 350 ms to about 1950 ms with a peak near 1100 ms. A dashed line marks the 1.2 s deadline near the middle of the distribution.}{\input{generated/bakery-durations.tex}}
\caption{Duration of every recorded sale. The mean sits just inside the
deadline; almost half of the sales do not.}
\label{fig:bakery-durations}
\end{bakeryfigure}

This is why a measured overlay must eventually carry distributions, not just
means, as FizzBee's performance files do: the deadline question is about the
spread, and a point estimate answers it with false confidence. Until then, the
raw-data column keeps the model honest by comparing its prediction with the
recorded fraction.

\section{Money and waste}

The same corpus answers economic questions through ordinary reducers. For each
calendar day, net revenue is the sum of \texttt{SaleCompleted} amounts less
refunds; ingredient cost comes from \texttt{BatchBaked}; employee pay from
\texttt{ShiftPaid}; and modeled contribution is revenue less ingredients and
pay. Donated bread is already in ingredient cost, so its value is reported
separately rather than subtracted twice, and it is not a tax saving until tax
rules are supplied. Costs that have no events in the corpus are excluded, so
this is contribution, not business profit.

Grouping the same events differently---by product, batch age, shift, or
order volume---gives further charts without changing the corpus. The axes are
always named reductions over events, which is what lets a measured overlay and
an economic chart come from one source of truth. How these specifications drive
code generation is the subject of Chapter~\ref{ch:bootstrap-contract}.
