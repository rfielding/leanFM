Chapter 1

Project sketch

GoServer is a KGS-inspired online Go service focused on multiplayer variants rather than direct
competition with established two-player Go servers. The initial release centers on short, four-
participant games played on an 11 × 11 board.
    KGS provides inspiration for the navigation model, but this sketch does not require compati-
bility with KGS accounts, protocols, clients, or server internals.


1.1    People and systems
The primary participant is a signed-in person who wants to browse chat rooms, find or observe
games, play Go, and converse with other participants. A game seat may instead be controlled by
a server-supported bot. People and bots are both game participants for the purposes of turn order
and team membership.
    The server maintains identities, sessions, rooms, games, board state, clocks, team membership,
participant graveyards, and chat history. Connected clients present server-authoritative state and
submit participant actions.


1.2    Primary navigation
After a successful login, a person sees the available chat rooms. Entering a room displays the
room conversation and the games associated with that room. Entering a game displays its board,
participants, teams, move deadline, current turn, game status, and game-specific chat.
   The intended hierarchy is:

  1. authenticate;

  2. browse and enter a chat room;

  3. inspect the games associated with that room;

  4. enter a game as a player or observer; and

  5. play or observe while using the game chat.

   Room chat and game chat are separate conversations. A message sent to one is not implicitly
copied to the other.

1.3     Initial game format
The first supported format has the following fixed profile:

    an 11 × 11 Go board;

    four participant seats;

    sequential turns;

    a ten-second deadline for each move;

    each seat configurable as either a person or a bot; and

    each participant initially occupying a distinct team.

    The server is authoritative for legal moves, turn order, deadlines, captures, repetition rules,
team membership, graveyard contents, and the final result. Clients must not resolve conflicting
game actions locally.
    When a stone is placed, the server first examines its orthogonally adjacent groups. Every
adjacent group that has no remaining liberties is removed before the server evaluates the placed
stone for suicide. This ordering permits an otherwise surrounded placement to survive when it first
captures neighboring stones and thereby gains liberties.
    If no neighboring group is removed and the placed stone’s connected same-owner group has no
liberties, the move is a permitted suicide move. The server removes the newly placed stone and
its entire connected same-owner group. This permits multiplayer tactics in which a participant
removes their own group to help an allied participant form eyes. Every self-removed stone is placed
in its owner’s graveyard, just like a stone removed by another participant.


1.4     Teams and alliances
Every participant begins the game as the sole member of a separate team. Participants may
subsequently join together so that multiple participants belong to one team. A team therefore
represents an alliance of one or more seats rather than a single shared seat.
    A team merge changes cooperation and scoring without removing a participant’s seat or turn.
Each participant continues to place stones during that participant’s assigned turns. Existing stones
remain on the board when teams merge.
    For each participant, the server maintains a graveyard containing that participant’s stones that
have been removed from play. When, for example, White captures a Black stone, the removed stone
is given to the Black participant’s graveyard rather than credited to White. A stone removed by
its owner’s suicide is likewise placed in that owner’s graveyard. Removal therefore does not directly
award points to another participant.
    A team’s score is its total territory minus its total losses. Total losses are the number of removed
stones in the graveyards of the team’s members. Equivalently, if AT is the territory attributed to
team T and GT is the combined number of stones in its members’ graveyards, then

                                           ST = AT − GT .

This makes the result express territory gained at the cost of stones lost. The approval, timing, and
reversibility of a team merge remain requirements decisions.

1.5     Bots
Each of the four seats can be assigned independently to a person or bot. This permits any mixture
from four people to four bots. Computer-controlled seats use Monte Carlo tree search executed by
the server. Clients do not run the authoritative bot search or submit bot-selected moves on behalf
of a bot seat.
    A bot is subject to the same legal-move checks and ten-second move deadline as a person. Its
server-side search must stop when its turn expires, and a result that arrives after the authoritative
deadline must not advance the game. Bot failures must not give the bot permission to bypass the
clock, acquire privileges unavailable to its assigned seat, or submit moves after its turn has ended.
The implementation must bound and isolate bot computation so that one search cannot exhaust
resources needed by unrelated games.


1.6     Chat
A participant can converse in a room without entering a game. Every game also has a dedicated
chat visible to people who have entered that game, subject to the eventual observer and moderation
policy. Bot participation in chat is not required by the initial sketch.


1.7     Representative scenarios
1.7.1   Find and enter a game
A person logs in, sees the room list, enters a room, and receives the current room conversation
and game list. The person selects a game and receives its board, participants, teams, clock state,
graveyard counts, and game chat.

1.7.2   Play a timed move
When a participant’s turn begins, the server starts a ten-second deadline. The participant submits
a board coordinate. The server accepts the move only if the participant controls the active seat,
the turn is still current, the deadline has not expired, and the move is legal. The accepted state is
then distributed to everyone viewing the game.

1.7.3   Capture neighboring groups
A participant places a stone next to one or more groups that have lost their final liberty. The server
removes all such neighboring groups before evaluating whether the newly placed stone’s group has
liberties. Each removed stone is placed in the graveyard of the participant who owned it. No
capture point is awarded to the participant who placed the stone.

1.7.4   Play a suicide move
A participant places a stone that does not remove any neighboring group, and the placed stone’s
connected same-owner group is left without liberties. The server treats the move as a permitted
suicide and removes the placed stone together with its entire connected same-owner group. Every
self-removed stone is placed in its owner’s graveyard and therefore contributes to that owner’s
losses.

1.7.5   Fill a seat with a bot
A game is configured with one or more bot-controlled seats. When a bot-controlled turn begins,
the server runs that bot’s Monte Carlo tree search within server-controlled execution. The search
operates under the same ten-second deadline and move-legality constraints as a human-controlled
seat. Only a result selected before the authoritative deadline may be submitted for the current
turn.

1.7.6   Form an alliance
Participants who began on separate teams request to become members of the same team. The
server applies the selected merge policy, records the resulting membership, and distributes the
updated team state to all viewers. Existing stones remain on the board. The resulting team’s loss
total includes the graveyards of all its members.

1.7.7   Score a team
At the end of a game, the server totals the territory attributed to a team and the removed stones
held in its members’ graveyards. It subtracts the graveyard total from the territory total and
reports the resulting team score. Captures and suicides affect scoring only as losses borne by the
owners of the removed stones.

1.7.8   Chat inside a game
A person viewing a game sends a message to the game chat. The server associates the message
with that game and distributes it to authorized game viewers without adding it to the containing
room’s chat.

Requirements argument

2.1    Observable scope
The candidate first-release requirements will cover authentication, room discovery, room entry,
room chat, room-associated game discovery, game entry, game chat, four-seat game setup, human
and bot seats, server-executed bot search, server-authoritative timed turns, multiplayer Go rules,
participant graveyards, team merges, scoring, reconnect behavior, and observable error responses.
    Account registration, moderation administration, persistent ratings, tournaments, KGS proto-
col compatibility, and sophisticated bot strategy beyond the required server-side Monte Carlo tree
search are not yet part of the committed first-release scope.


2.2    Proposed safety and consistency invariants
The requirements should preserve the following positive invariant: every externally visible room,
chat, game, move, clock, capture, graveyard, score, and team transition is authorized for the acting
identity and derived from one unambiguous server-authoritative state.
    Candidate forbidden behaviors include accepting a move from the wrong seat, accepting more
than one move for a turn, accepting a move after its deadline, resolving suicide before neighbor-
ing captures, removing less or more than the entire connected same-owner group during suicide,
crediting a removed stone to another participant, placing a removed stone in the wrong owner’s
graveyard, allowing a client to manufacture team membership or graveyard contents, accepting a
client-supplied move as the output of a server-controlled bot, leaking private session credentials
into chat or logs, crossing room or game boundaries through an identifier substitution, letting a
bot acquire privileges unavailable to its assigned seat, and allowing one bot search to consume
unbounded server resources.
    These statements are design candidates rather than accepted requirements until represented by
the typed project specification and explicitly accepted.


2.3    Resolved capture and suicide ordering
After a stone is placed, adjacent groups without liberties are removed first. Only if no neighboring
group is removed does the server evaluate the placement as suicide. Thus a placement that captures
a neighboring group can gain the liberties created by that removal and remain on the board.
   Suicide is permitted as a multiplayer tactic, including to help an ally form eyes. If the placed
stone’s connected same-owner group has no liberties, the server removes the placed stone and that

entire connected group. All self-removed stones enter their respective owners’ graveyards and count
as losses in exactly the same way as stones removed by another participant.


2.4     Resolved scoring direction
A removed stone is assigned to the graveyard of the participant who owned it. It counts as one loss
for that participant and is not a positive capture point for another participant.
    A team’s final score is its total territory minus the combined number of stones in its members’
graveyards. Graveyard ownership remains attached to the participant, allowing team losses to be
recomputed from authoritative participant records after a team merge. The typed specification
must still define how territory is identified and attributed.


2.5     Resolved bot execution direction
Computer-player Monte Carlo tree search runs on the server rather than in a connected client. The
authoritative game clock bounds the search, and a late search result cannot advance an expired or
superseded turn. The implementation mapping must define resource bounds and execution isolation
for simultaneous searches without changing the observable ten-second deadline.


2.6     Recovered ko and repetition behavior
The supplied N-Player Go Lab JavaScript provides concrete provenance for the intended ko be-
havior. Its user-facing description calls the rule “simple ko plus positional superko,” but its state
comparison includes both the board and the participant to move. The standard description of the
implemented rule is therefore situational superko, not positional superko.
   The candidate first-release baseline is as follows:

    the ko rule is enabled by default for a two-participant game and disabled by default for a
     game with three or more participants;

    when enabled, a qualifying single-stone capture establishes a simple-ko restriction that pre-
     vents the owner of the captured stone from immediately recapturing at that point on that
     participant’s next turn;

    after every proposed stone placement, the resulting board together with the next participant
     to move must differ from every earlier such state on the current game line;

    passes, deadline expirations, and resignations are not rejected merely because they leave or
     recreate a repeated state; and

    independently of repetition, a game is capped at 3N 2 moves for an N × N board.

    The prototype represents a repetition state with a 32-bit Zobrist board hash combined with
a token for the next participant. That representation is useful evidence of semantics but is not
sufficient as the sole authoritative equality test because a hash collision could reject a legal position
as repeated. The production requirement should demand collision-safe comparison, such as an
exact canonical state representation or a digest backed by exact equality when hashes match.
    The repetition check applies to actual game-tree branches and is mirrored inside Monte Carlo
tree search: a searched stone placement is skipped when its resulting board-and-next-participant

state occurs in the real game history or in the current search path. This search behavior is advisory;
the authoritative game transition must independently enforce the same observable rule.


2.7     Open rules decisions
The remaining core multiplayer rules still need durable answers for:

    whether the ko rule is fixed for the first-release four-participant format or configurable when
     a game is created;

    whether an expired deadline causes an automatic pass, forfeits the seat, or has another result;

    how territory is identified and attributed among participants and teams;

    when team merges are allowed, who must approve them, and whether they are reversible;

    whether observers may use game chat; and

    what reconnect grace, if any, exists within a ten-second turn.

Implementation argument

Implementation is blocked until the observable behavior is encoded as a typed LeanFM require-
ment specification, checked, and explicitly accepted, followed by a checked and explicitly accepted
implementation mapping.
    Once those gates are satisfied, the implementation argument must map authentication, autho-
rization, room isolation, game isolation, clocks, turn serialization, legal-move validation, ordered
capture and suicide processing, graveyard attribution, collision-safe situational-superko validation,
team transitions, server-side Monte Carlo tree search, bot resource isolation, persistence, chat de-
livery, and scoring attribution to concrete controls and tests. In particular, concurrent move and
timeout processing must have a single atomic winner so that a deadline and a move cannot both
advance the same turn. A bot result must additionally be bound to its game, seat, turn revision,
and deadline so that a delayed or confused result cannot be applied to another turn. A placement,
all neighboring removals, any resulting connected-group suicide removal, repetition validation, and
all corresponding board, graveyard, and score-relevant changes must be committed as one author-
itative transition.

Current reduced server-only scope

The operator explicitly selected formalization and checking of the reduced basic-server slice in
question basic_server_requirements_gate. The current requirements cover room discovery and
entry, room game/challenge listings, named open challenge creation, watch/join navigation, room
chat and per-game chat. A game is currently a navigable chat container. Earlier chapters’ Go
boards, move rules, bots, clocks, teams and scoring remain future roadmap material and are not
obligations of this first server-only requirements gate.
    Existing server-issued sessions identify callers. Joining a room or game establishes its chat
membership; sender identity and timestamps come from the server. Chat is never implicitly copied
across room/game boundaries. The draft adds request correlation and explicit rejection branches
without choosing an implementation runtime or HTTP transport. Watch/join confers no Go move
or seat authority in this slice. Account registration is not added.
    Proposed reversible bounds are 128 characters for names, 4000 for chat text and the latest
100 chat entries per conversation. The retained 100-person population is a capacity forecast,
not measured performance. Rejected or overloaded actions must not mutate state or disclose
private identities or credentials. Replay and concurrency controls, connection limits, persistence
and negative tests must be realized in the separate implementation mapping after requirements
acceptance.
    Canonical candidates are spec/Requirements.lean and spec/Requirements.proto. Checked
task views are generated from that same specification into spec/tasks.json; the task catalog
contains one interaction diagram and state machine for each scoped operation. No human gate is
accepted by compilation or by preparing these files.
